📚 Theory for Translators

Simplified explanation from student to student according research and experience

🎭

1. Multimodality and semiotics

A meme is not simply text accompanied by an image; it is a unified "multicode" entity. Translating only the words is pointless, as meaning arises at the intersection of these elements.

Multimodality (Kress, Leeuwen, 2010) studies external form and structure (how text, colour, font and image physically interact).

Semiotics studies deeper meaning (cultural codes, ideology, intertextuality). Dawkins (2016) regarded memes as blind copies, whilst Shifman (2013) demonstrated that in the digital age, they represent a conscious remix and a social statement by the creator.

🧩

2. Functions of text

According to Barthes, 1977; Attardo, 2020 and McCloud, 2022:

Text can function as an "Anchorage" - limiting the meaning of an image (by guiding perception), or as a "Relay" - moving the narrative forward by complementing the image rather than explaining it.

Why this division (Verbal, Visual, Verbal-Visual + Translingual):

We categorise transformations by channel to understand exactly where the shift occurs during translation (text, images, or a combination of both), also relying on ideas of Izgarsheva (2022) — this clearly shows which component (or combination) bears the main burden of adapting the meme.

Translingual: These are placed in a separate category because they are not translated in the strict sense at all — memes that migrate between cultures in their original form as shared digital symbols with minimal differences in perception.

🎯

3. Skopos

The Skopos theory (Reiss, Vermeer, Nord, 2014) holds that the most important aspect of translation is not literal fidelity to the original, but the achievement of the communicative purpose within the target culture.

What this means for memes:

Memes function through pragmatics. Skopos gives the translator free rein: it allows them to alter the text, the template or the cultural references in order to preserve the intended emotional effect. A successful translation of a meme is one that makes people laugh (or cry) just as much as the original, even if the words are completely different.

😊

4. Ekman's basic emotions

Memes are consumed whilst scrolling quickly, so Ekman's classification (Ekman, 1992; 2003) is ideal thanks to its principles:

Rapid onset & automatic appraisal — the visual part of the meme hits biological triggers before the brain deeply analyses the context. A clear and rapid signal.

Refractory period: Emotion distorts perception. Memes exploit this effect; if a meme resonates with the user's current emotional state and is reinforced by authorities (e.g. a favourite blogger), the brain accepts it as truth.

Memes were classified across:

😠 Anger

😨 Fear

😢 Sadness

😲 Surprise

🤢 Disgust

😁 Joy

💡 However, the triggers for these emotions vary across cultures.
🔄

5. Translation Strategies

Existing translation classifications are too fragmented for memes. A working triad is proposed, based on the recognisability of the underlying scenario:

Direct transfer: Minimal changes; the form is retained.

Adaptation: The original scenario is recognisable, but has been reworded or culturally adapted for a new audience.

Recreation: The original scenario is completely replaced; only the template or the general logic of the humour remains.
